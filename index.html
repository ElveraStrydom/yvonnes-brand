Rebrand · November 2026

Yvonne’s: three directions

One logo, one slogan, one pair of typefaces, and three colour directions to choose between. Each is shown on its cream ground and reversed on its own colour, in landscape and square.

01 · The slogan

Time-honoured recipes

Three lines were on the table. One of them does a different job from the other two, and that decides it.

  • Artisanal bakesTells a stranger what the business is. Useful, but it is a label rather than a slogan, and “artisanal” is the most worn-out word in food. Keep it as a small descriptor if ever needed, not as the line.
  • Traditionally craftedHits craftsmanship, but it is interchangeable with a hundred other bakeries and slightly stiff to say out loud.
  • Time-honoured recipesThe warmest of the three, the best rhythm, and the one that points at the real story: a handwritten cookbook passed from mother to daughter. Hyphenated, because “time-honoured” is one idea.
Time-honoured recipes carries heritage and warmth without explaining itself. The new recipes still belong under it, because they are made the time-honoured way, in the same kitchen tradition.

A line to keep in reserve for packaging or social, if a second voice is ever wanted: Recipes worth handing down. It honours Yvonne’s recipes and quietly promises the new ones will earn the same place.

02 · The typefaces

A book face and a handwritten one

Yvonne’s
Libre Caslon for the name

Caslon is an eighteenth-century English book type, the face of old cookbooks, newspapers and shop fascias. Its strokes are sturdy rather than hairline, so the name survives being shrunk to a WhatsApp profile picture or printed on a box sticker. Set in spaced capitals, it reads as established and permanent, the way lettering over a shop door does.

Time-honoured recipes
Caveat for the slogan

Handwriting, not calligraphy. The earlier scripts looked like a wedding invitation; this looks like a note in the margin of a recipe. It stays legible at small sizes and gives the logo its warmth and human hand. The best version of this logo replaces it with a line in Yvonne’s own handwriting, scanned from the cookbook.

The pairing is the idea in miniature: a formal, long-lived name above an informal, personal line. Quality and heritage from the serif; warmth and authenticity from the hand.

03 · The colours

One cream, three inks

The background stays the same across all three directions. Cream is butter, flour, parchment and the yellowed page of an old notebook. It is warm without competing with the food, and it is the ground forecasters expect to replace grey in 2026. Each direction then changes only the ink, which is what gives the brand its character.

Why not brown

Brown on cream says handcrafted and traditional, which is exactly why every home bakery uses it. The brief asks for warmth and heritage, but a logo also has to be hers. Each of the three inks below keeps the heritage feeling while standing apart from the brown-and-kraft crowd.

Why these three

All three clear the strictest contrast standard for large lettering on their cream, so they will hold up on a sticker, a bag or a phone. Their depth is deliberate: research on logo colour shows darker logos make a brand feel older and lighter ones younger, so each ink sits in the middle of the scale, dark enough to read as established, light enough not to read as dated. The set covers one warm, one cool and one bright direction.

The ink

Jam, red wine, a leather-bound book. Burgundy is warm, rich and old, which is the whole brief in one colour. It is almost never used by home bakeries, so it is distinctive without trying to be.

The ground

Standard cream. Burgundy and cream is a documented “richer tones” pairing for bakeries and reads like a preserve label from a good delicatessen.

Reversed

Cream lettering on burgundy is the strongest dark version of the three: it looks like foil-stamped packaging and suits ribbon, boxes and gift tags.

Best if the brand should feel warm first and distinctive second.

The ink

Enamelware, old kitchen tiles, an apothecary jar. Deep teal is the forecasters’ anchor colour for 2026 and the most distinctive of the three. It is calm and confident rather than warm; the warmth comes from the cream, the handwriting and photographs of the bakes.

The ground

Standard cream. Teal is specifically recommended on cream rather than white, which softens it and keeps it from reading as corporate or clinical.

Reversed

Cream on teal has a mid-century grocer feel. Pair it with a single warm accent, such as terracotta or mustard, on packaging so the whole set stays inviting.

Best if standing out matters more than feeling cosy.

The ink

The blue of Delft pottery and old enamel kitchenware: cobalt’s heritage cousin. Cobalt is the defining colour of 2026 across fashion and interiors, and this is the version of it that still belongs in a kitchen. It is lively where navy is heavy, and it flatters golden-brown bakes by contrast.

The ground

A slightly lighter, less yellow cream. Designers pair cobalt with whites and warm whites rather than butter tones, and Delft pottery itself is blue on a pale glaze. This cream keeps the blue bright and the lettering crisp.

Reversed

Cream on Delft blue looks like a painted shop sign or a tin of biscuits. It is the brightest of the three dark versions and works well as a sticker on kraft paper.

Best if the brand should feel fresh and current while still looking like it has a history.
