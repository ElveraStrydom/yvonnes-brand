Rebrand · November 2026

Yvonne’s: five directions

One logo, one slogan, one pair of typefaces, and three recommended colour directions and two more that were also considered. Each is shown on its own cream and reversed on its main colour, in landscape and square, with the full palette underneath, followed by how to take it to a printer and test it with people.

01 · The slogan

Time-honoured recipes

Three lines were on the table. One of them does a different job from the other two, and that decides it.

  • Artisanal bakesTells a stranger what the business is. Useful, but it is a label rather than a slogan, and “artisanal” is the most worn-out word in food. Keep it as a small descriptor if ever needed, not as the line.
  • Traditionally craftedHits craftsmanship, but it is interchangeable with a hundred other bakeries and slightly stiff to say out loud.
  • Time-honoured recipesThe warmest of the three, the best rhythm, and the one that points at the real story: a handwritten cookbook passed from mother to daughter. Hyphenated, because “time-honoured” is one idea.
Time-honoured recipes carries heritage and warmth without explaining itself. The new recipes still belong under it, because they are made the time-honoured way, in the same kitchen tradition.

A line to keep in reserve for packaging or social, if a second voice is ever wanted: Recipes worth handing down. It honours Yvonne’s recipes and quietly promises the new ones will earn the same place.

02 · The typefaces

A book face and a handwritten one

Yvonne’s
Libre Caslon for the name

Caslon is an eighteenth-century English book type, the face of old cookbooks, newspapers and shop fascias. Its strokes are sturdy rather than hairline, so the name survives being shrunk to a WhatsApp profile picture or printed on a box sticker. Set in spaced capitals, it reads as established and permanent, the way lettering over a shop door does.

Time-honoured recipes
Caveat for the slogan

Handwriting, not calligraphy. The earlier scripts looked like a wedding invitation; this looks like a note in the margin of a recipe. It stays legible at small sizes and gives the logo its warmth and human hand. The best version of this logo replaces it with a line in Yvonne’s own handwriting, scanned from the cookbook.

The pairing is the idea in miniature: a formal, long-lived name above an informal, personal line. Quality and heritage from the serif; warmth and authenticity from the hand.

03 · The colours

Five directions, five colours each

Each direction is a small family rather than a single ink. Every family has the same five jobs, so whichever one is chosen, it is clear which colour goes where.

Main colourThe logo, headings and packaging. This is the colour people will remember.
BackgroundMost of every page, box and post. A cream chosen to suit that main colour.
SecondaryPanels, tissue paper and backgrounds for photos. A softer cousin of the main colour.
AccentA small touch: a line, a stamp, a ribbon. Used sparingly, it is what catches the eye.
Text colourBody text and fine print. A very dark shade, warmer and softer than black.
How much of each

Designers often balance a palette roughly 60, 30, 10: background most of the time, the main and secondary colours for about a third, and the accent for the last tenth. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, but it is why the accent should stay small.

One rule for words

Put words only in the main colour or the text colour. The secondary and accent colours are too light to read on the cream, so use them for shapes, lines and wrapping, never for writing. On a secondary-colour panel, write in the text colour.

The top three

One warm, one cool, one natural

These three are the clearest to read and the most different from each other, so they make a fair choice. Each has a story a customer understands at a glance: a family cookbook, Delft pottery, a country kitchen.

04 · Taking it to the printer

Judge the colour on the real thing

A screen makes colour with light and a printer makes it with ink, so the same colour can come out duller or darker on paper. Decide only from the printer’s proof, never from a phone or a home printer.

  1. Bring the print version and the colour codes. Ask the printer to match each main colour to its nearest Pantone colour. The printer converts the colours on their own machines, so let them do it rather than bringing your own print numbers.
  2. Ask for proofs on the actual material. The box board, the sticker paper, the kraft bag. Shiny paper, matte paper and brown kraft each change how a colour looks.
  3. Print every direction on the same items. The same box and the same sticker for each, so the colour is the only thing that changes.
  4. Look at the proofs in daylight, and in the light where the bakes will be sold.
  5. Write down the Pantone codes the printer used for the winner, next to its colour codes, and ask for those same codes every time.
05 · Testing it with people

Ask people who would buy from her

  1. Show the printed packaging, not a screen. People react differently to a logo on a real box than to a picture of it.
  2. Ask people like her customers. Friends and family are kind, not neutral. Customers and people who buy baked goods are the ones who count.
  3. Put them all out together, in a different order for each person, and do not say which one you like.
  4. Ask “Which one would you pick up, and why?” Not “Which looks more homely?”, because naming a word nudges people towards it.
  5. Then ask for three words from a list that mixes good and bad: warm, old-fashioned, elegant, dull, fresh, cheap, homemade, expensive. The words people choose say more than the vote.
  6. Write down the reasons, not only the pick. Fifteen to thirty people is a sensible rough number: enough to see a pattern, not proof.
